The cost of living in Thailand for one foreign resident usually starts around THB 25,000–40,000 a month for a modest setup and reaches THB 50,000–90,000 for a more comfortable lifestyle in a major expat city. Chiang Mai sits near the lower end, Bangkok costs more when you live close to rapid transit, and Phuket can exceed Bangkok once beach-area rent and transport are included. These are planning ranges for a single person renting long term, not official averages or promises.

The figures below were checked on 29 October 2026 and are shown in Thai baht because that is how rent and bills are paid. For rough conversion, this guide uses THB 33.75 to USD 1, derived from the European Central Bank reference rates for 28 October 2026. Exchange rates move, so build your working budget in baht and add a currency buffer if your income arrives in dollars, euros or pounds.

What a useful Thailand budget must include

A national average hides the decisions that actually determine your monthly spending. Build the budget from the apartment outward:

  1. Choose the exact city and neighbourhood. Bangkok next to a central BTS station is not comparable with outer Chiang Mai or a provincial town.
  2. Use a real lease length. A six- or twelve-month agreement usually prices differently from a one-month stay. A hotel or serviced apartment is not a long-term rent benchmark.
  3. Set a food pattern. Thai meals and local produce keep spending low; imported groceries, wine and frequent Western restaurants can move the total quickly.
  4. Estimate electricity from air-conditioning use. Floor area alone is not enough. Sun exposure, insulation, thermostat setting and hours at home matter.
  5. Add costs that are not monthly. Deposits, visa fees, insurance, flights home, dental work and replacing electronics need their own annual or emergency reserve.

Rent: the largest and least transferable number

Rent is usually the biggest line in an expat budget, but online averages are only a reference. Numbeo’s 2026 city data places a one-bedroom apartment at roughly THB 9,500 outside central Chiang Mai and THB 16,500 in the centre. The comparable reference is about THB 10,600 outside central Bangkok and THB 21,900 in the centre. Phuket is higher at about THB 15,600 outside the centre and THB 23,500 centrally. Current Pattaya data is around THB 13,500 and THB 20,500 respectively.

Those figures are crowdsourced averages, not live offers. A newer building, short walk to transit, sea view, modern kitchen or high season can push a listing above them. An older unit farther from transport may cost less but create a second expense through taxis, a scooter or a car.

Before signing, ask who bills electricity and water, whether the advertised price requires a long lease, how the deposit is returned and what condition report will be signed. Treat advance rent, the security deposit, moving costs and basic household purchases as setup costs rather than hiding them inside the first monthly budget.

Electricity, water, internet and mobile service

Thailand’s Energy Regulatory Commission publishes progressive residential electricity rates. For households using more than 150 kWh a month, the base rate is THB 3.2484 per kWh for the first 150 kWh, THB 4.2218 for the next 250 kWh and THB 4.4217 above 400 kWh. The bill also includes the current fuel-adjustment charge and VAT. For September–December 2026, the published Ft is THB 0.1623 per kWh.

This is why a fixed “utilities cost” is unreliable. A small condo used mainly in the evening can be inexpensive; a large west-facing unit with air-conditioning running all day will not be. Also confirm that the landlord charges the utility tariff rather than a higher private per-unit rate.

Outside Bangkok, the Provincial Waterworks Authority lists residential water at THB 10.20 per cubic metre for the first 10 m³, then THB 16 for the next band. Water itself is normally a small part of the budget. Internet and a mobile plan are more predictable, but compare installation, router and contract terms before treating an introductory price as permanent.

Food: local habits make a larger difference than the headline average

A realistic single-person food budget can range from about THB 8,000 to THB 18,000 a month. The lower end assumes frequent Thai meals, local markets and limited alcohol. The upper end allows more international groceries, cafés, delivery and Western restaurants. Premium imports can take spending beyond that range even in Chiang Mai.

Do not compare a tourist’s street-food challenge with a resident’s full grocery bill. Long-term spending includes coffee, household supplies, drinking water, occasional delivery fees and the products you keep buying after the novelty wears off. Track two ordinary weeks during a trial stay, then multiply and add a margin for social meals.

Transport costs depend on whether the neighbourhood works without a vehicle

Bangkok can support a car-free routine when home and work sit near BTS or MRT lines. The rent premium may be partly offset by avoiding vehicle costs. Chiang Mai, Phuket, Hua Hin and many parts of Pattaya often require more reliance on ride-hailing, songthaews, a scooter or a car.

A scooter budget is not just rental and fuel. Include a valid licence, suitable insurance, helmet, maintenance and the financial risk of an accident. If you do not want to drive, price several normal weekly routes in the exact neighbourhood before choosing cheaper housing far from shops and services.

Healthcare and insurance do not fit one national average

Routine private consultations can be affordable compared with the United States, but that does not make major treatment predictable. Insurance pricing depends on age, medical history, deductible, geographic coverage and whether outpatient care is included. A young remote worker and a retiree with chronic conditions should not share the same placeholder.

Ask insurers for written quotes and exclusions before committing to a city or visa route. Keep a separate medical reserve even with insurance. People choosing an island or smaller city should also consider travel to Bangkok or another regional hospital for specialist care.

Costs that cheap-Thailand budgets often omit

  • Lease setup: advance rent, security deposit, household items and temporary accommodation while viewing properties.
  • Immigration: application or extension fees, photos, translations, travel to an office and any required financial evidence. Thailand’s Immigration Bureau lists THB 1,900 for many extension applications, but that is not the total cost of qualifying for a visa or status.
  • Insurance and healthcare: premiums, exclusions, deductibles, dental work and medicines.
  • Travel: flights home, domestic trips and higher transport spending during smoke season or severe weather.
  • Currency risk: a 10% move in the baht changes the foreign-currency cost of every local bill.
  • Tax and banking: transfer fees and professional advice where residence or income creates obligations.

How much cash to plan for the first month

A sensible relocation fund is larger than one month of ordinary spending. For a long-term rental, start with this formula:

Do not transfer a large deposit or prepay a long lease only to hit a target budget. Confirm ownership or agent authority, inspect the property, record its condition and understand the exit terms.

What monthly budget should you test?

Test THB 30,000–40,000 if you are single, flexible on neighbourhood, eat mostly local food and choose Chiang Mai or another lower-cost city. This range leaves little room for expensive insurance, frequent flights or imported habits.

Test THB 50,000–65,000 for a comfortable single lifestyle in Chiang Mai, Pattaya or Hua Hin, or a careful Bangkok budget. It gives more choice in housing and leisure but still requires trade-offs in central Bangkok and Phuket.

Test THB 70,000–90,000+ if you want a central modern condo, regular international dining, private health cover, frequent ride-hailing or an island lifestyle. Families should build from actual school and insurance quotes rather than multiplying a single-person number.

Read the Thailand relocation guide, compare cities in the city comparison tool, then spend a normal month in the chosen neighbourhood. Record rent alternatives, electricity use, food and transport before deciding that the country fits your long-term budget.
