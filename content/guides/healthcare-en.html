Healthcare in Asia: choose insurance and hospitals before moving

Good healthcare in a country does not prove that it is accessible to you, close to your city or paid by your policy. Plan three layers: eligibility, a real provider network and financial protection.

Quick answer

Before moving, identify a 24-hour clinic, a tertiary hospital and a method of payment. Confirm access to the public system, any visa-linked insurance requirement, and whether an international plan covers your residence country, the nearest treatment hub and your home country.

Read exclusions before the headline limit

  1. Pre-existing conditions: excluded, loaded or covered after a wait?
  2. Inpatient and outpatient: are routine doctors, diagnostics and prescriptions included?
  3. Direct billing: does the insurer pay the hospital or reimburse you?
  4. Evacuation: who chooses the hospital and is an escort covered?
  5. Maternity and mental health: what waits and sublimits apply?
  6. Renewal: can pricing or terms change after a major diagnosis?

Travel insurance is generally designed for emergencies during a trip and repatriation. It is not the same as long-term everyday medical cover.

Country and city are separate decisions

Major hubs

Singapore, Bangkok, Kuala Lumpur, Tokyo, Seoul and Hong Kong offer broad choice, but prices and eligibility differ.

Islands and smaller cities

Routine care may be adequate while complex treatment requires a flight. Check evacuation and weather-related transport limits.

Family

Map paediatrics, maternity, vaccination, emergency care and language support before choosing a neighbourhood.

Build a medical folder

  • short diagnosis and surgery history;
  • medicines by international non-proprietary name;
  • prescriptions and a doctor letter for controlled medicines;
  • vaccination records, tests and imaging;
  • insurer contacts and policy number;
  • emergency contacts and allergies in English or the local language.

Check medicine-import rules in advance. A medicine sold freely at home may require approval or be prohibited at destination.

Verify the hospital and the bill

International accreditation is one signal, not proof that the specialist is available or insurance will pay. Call the provider and ask about direct billing, deposits, interpreters, clinician availability and appointment time. For planned treatment, request a written estimate covering the doctor, theatre, anaesthesia, implants and medicines.

Common questions

Is employer insurance enough?

Check dependants, termination, geographic area, limits and portability after a job change.

Do I need evacuation cover?

It matters particularly on islands and where complex surgery is unavailable. Confirm destination and medical triggers.

Can I buy cover after a diagnosis?

You may apply, but the new condition can be excluded or individually underwritten. Never hide medical history.

Destination worksheet

Compare the healthcare you can actually use

For each candidate city, record the distance and realistic travel time to emergency care, a hospital with the required specialty, and a pharmacy carrying equivalent medicines. Add the appointment language, public eligibility, cash deposit, insurer network status and the route for a second opinion. A national healthcare ranking cannot answer these local questions.

Run two cases. The routine case covers a GP visit, blood tests, repeat prescriptions and dental care. The serious case covers admission, imaging, surgery, intensive care and rehabilitation. Ask what must be paid before admission and whether pre-authorisation is available twenty-four hours a day. If the insurer reimburses later, the emergency reserve needs to cover the hospital's deposit.

For a chronic condition, request continuity rather than only an annual limit. Confirm specialist availability, medicine registration, laboratory monitoring and whether a stable condition is still considered pre-existing. If a medicine name differs, use the active ingredient and dosage; do not substitute without a clinician.

Before and after arrival

A practical sequence

  1. Before applying for a visa, identify any mandatory policy wording or coverage amount.
  2. Before buying insurance, submit the full medical history and obtain written underwriting terms.
  3. Before flying, arrange enough medicine and check import approval.
  4. During the first week, register with a nearby clinic and test the insurer's app or hotline.
  5. After any move or job change, update address and eligibility immediately.

Keep both digital and offline copies of policy documents. Family members should know which number to call, which hospital to use and where the payment card or guarantee letter is stored. In an emergency, a simple shared plan is more useful than a long policy document nobody can find.

Cost control

Budget beyond the insurance premium

Include the annual deductible, co-insurance, excluded routine care, dental work, prescriptions and any hospital deposit due before the insurer confirms payment. A low premium can be expensive when outpatient care is self-funded or the network is far from home.

Ask how claims are submitted, which currency is used and how long reimbursement normally takes. If treatment outside the country requires prior approval, record the process before it is needed. For families, compare shared and separate deductibles and check how newborn cover begins.

Review the plan after a move, marriage, pregnancy, diagnosis or job change. Do not cancel the old policy until final written terms are issued and the new coverage has started; a gap can turn a newly diagnosed condition into an exclusion.

Record the renewal date and request terms early. This leaves time to compare alternatives without losing continuity or accepting exclusions under deadline pressure.

Keep an annual allowance for preventive care outside the policy. Screening, vaccinations, eye care and dental treatment are easier to manage when planned rather than postponed until urgent.
